# Tense, Aspect, Mood: DNS Bhat vs Chomsky and Other Frameworks

> Created: 2026-11-17 · Compares Bhat's *The Prominence of Tense, Aspect and Mood* ([Book 32](../dnsbhat/32-the-prominence-of-tense-aspect-and-mood/)) with generative grammar and the typologists, with Japanese and Sanskrit as case studies

Bhat's claim is that each language makes one of tense, aspect or mood its backbone, and that Kannada's backbone is tense. Chomsky's tradition instead gives every clause the same tense-and-mood skeleton and lets languages differ only in what they pronounce. The typologists (Comrie, Dahl, Bybee) sit between the two: the building blocks are universal, but languages pick and combine them differently.

For Kannada the practical upshot is Bhat's: past vs non-past is obligatory and runs through every verb form, while aspect (vector verbs) and mood (auxiliaries like ಬೇಕು, ಬಹುದು) are optional add-ons. A grammar built on Sanskrit's three tenses or English's nine misdescribes it.

## Bhat's framework

Bhat's claim is that languages are not equal mixes of tense, aspect and mood. Each language grammaticalises one of the three more heavily than the others, and the rest of its grammar is organised around that choice. Kannada and the other Dravidian languages are his model tense-prominent case.

### The three categories

| Category | Bhat's definition | Sub-types he distinguishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tense | Where an event sits relative to a reference point | Deictic (relative to speech time) vs non-deictic (prior, simultaneous or posterior to another event) |
| Aspect | The internal temporal shape of an event | Perfective/imperfective; phasal (start, continue, complete, stop); quantificational (habitual, iterative, distributive); situational (lexical, Aktionsart) vs viewpoint (grammatical), after Carlota Smith (1991) |
| Mood | The speaker's attitude to whether the event is actual | Three parameters: epistemic judgement, evidentiality, deontic necessity; realis/irrealis; imperatives and hortatives |

### How prominence is measured

A category counts as prominent when it scores highest on four criteria:

1. **Grammaticalisation**: it is carried by bound morphology, not by optional words.
2. **Obligatoriness**: a finite verb cannot leave it unmarked.
3. **Systematicity**: it forms a closed, regular paradigm.
4. **Pervasiveness**: it shows up beyond the finite verb, in participles, converbs, nominalisations and non-verbal predicates.

The types are idealised and graded, so some languages fit more cleanly than others. The book gives no formal scoring procedure. The criteria are applied through worked examples.

### The classification

- **Tense-prominent**: Dravidian (Kannada, Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam).
- **Aspect-prominent**: Slavic, Mandarin, many Niger-Congo languages. Bhat also reclassifies Hindi and Marathi here, because their "past" is really a perfective.
- **Mood-prominent**: Muna (realis/irrealis), Ladakhi and other Tibeto-Burman languages, Kayardild, many Papuan languages.

### Features that travel with each type

- **Foregrounding**: tense-prominent languages carry the narrative mainline on finite past verbs and push background into participles and converbs. Aspect-prominent languages do the same job with perfective vs imperfective.
- **Verb classes and adjectives**: tense-prominent languages lean toward active (event) verbs and keep adjectives apart from verbs, or group them with nouns.
- **Marker order, grammaticalisation paths and split ergativity** are also tested against the typology. Chapter 7 also reads Chomsky's tensedness parameter as a scale rather than an on/off switch.

### The Kannada evidence

The Kannada evidence comes from Book 32 and from the paradigm counts in Book 07 (*ಕನ್ನಡ ಬರಹದ ಸೊಲ್ಲರಿಮೆ*, §5.1.1; see the [verb paradigms](./)).

- **Tense is obligatory and systematic.** Every finite form of ಮಾಡು is past or non-past, and all are part of one 36-form paradigm (23 finite, 13 non-finite). State verbs like ಇರು add a third tense, ಈಪೊತ್ತು (ಇದೆ / ಇವೆ), giving 42 forms.
- **Tense is pervasive.** It runs into the non-finite forms: the relative participles ಮಾಡುವ / ಮಾಡಿದ, the verbal nouns ಮಾಡುವುದು / ಮಾಡಿದುದು, and the three converbs ಮಾಡಿ (prior), ಮಾಡುತ್ತ (simultaneous) and ಮಾಡಲು (posterior). These converbs mark non-deictic tense.
- **Aspect is optional and lexical.** Perfectivity and phase come from vector verbs (ಮಾಡಿಬಿಡು, ಮಾಡಿಕೊಳ್ಳು, ಮಾಡುತ್ತಿರು), which a speaker can leave out.
- **Mood is periphrastic.** Ability, unwillingness, obligation and possibility are carried by auxiliaries outside the core paradigm: ಮಾಡಲಾರೆ, ಮಾಡಲೊಲ್ಲೆ, ಮಾಡಬಲ್ಲೆ, ಮಾಡಬೇಕು, ಮಾಡಬಹುದು.

Bhat's theoretical stance follows from this. Chapter 1 contrasts a *universalistic* approach, which he attributes to Greenberg and the Chomskyan tradition, with a *differentiating* one that treats cross-linguistic differences as real. He favours the second, though he uses both. Chapter 5's "red glass" analogy makes the practical point: a grammarian who looks through a tense-shaped lens will mis-see an aspect- or mood-prominent language, and the reverse.

## Chomsky and the generative tradition

Generative grammar treats TAM as positions in a universal clause skeleton, not as a typological choice. The theory's question is *where* tense, aspect and mood sit in the tree and what they do syntactically. Whether one of them dominates a language is not a question it asks.

### How the treatment developed

1. **Aux (1957)**: in *Syntactic Structures*, tense is an affix on the auxiliary that hops onto the verb ("Affix Hopping").
2. **INFL (1981)**: in *Lectures on Government and Binding*, the sentence becomes a projection of INFL, a head bundling ±Tense and agreement. Finite INFL (+Tense, AGR) assigns nominative case to the subject, so tense is tied to subjecthood and finiteness. This is the "tensedness" Bhat reads as a scale in his Chapter 7.
3. **Split INFL (Pollock 1989)**: INFL is broken into separate heads for tense, agreement and negation (TP > NegP > AgrP > VP). The evidence is verb-placement differences between French and English.
4. **Cartography (Cinque 1999)**: *Adverbs and Functional Heads* argues for one fixed universal hierarchy of about 30 functional heads for mood, modality, tense and aspect. Adverb order and suffix order match it across languages. Roughly: speech-act and evaluative mood > evidential > epistemic > tense (past, future) > irrealis > root modality > the aspects (habitual, repetitive, continuative, perfect, progressive, completive).
5. **Minimalism (1995 on)**: T is a probe whose uninterpretable features drive agreement and movement. Its semantic content matters less than its syntactic role.

### The generative answer to tenseless languages

The hardest cases for "every clause has T" are languages with no obligatory tense morphology. That is the same territory Bhat calls aspect- or mood-prominent. Ritter and Wiltschko (2009, 2014) keep the universal slot and vary its content:

- INFL has a universal *function*, anchoring the event to the utterance.
- Its *content* varies by language: time in English, location in Halkomelem (Salish), person in Blackfoot (Algonquian).

They call this the Parametric Substantiation Hypothesis. It is the closest the generative tradition comes to Bhat's prominence idea, because it admits that languages build their anchoring on different categories. The difference is that the slot is fixed by Universal Grammar, and only what fills it varies.

### What Bhat objects to

Bhat's quarrel is with the *method*, not with trees. A universalist grammar starts from a fixed set of categories, usually modelled on European languages, and looks for them everywhere. Bhat argues this is how Kannada and Manipuri were wrongly given European-style grammatical relations. On TAM in particular, a T-headed clause skeleton invites the analyst to find tense in a mood-prominent language even when tense marking there is optional.

## Other takes

Bhat sits inside the functional-typological tradition, not outside it. His one new move is to rank the three categories against each other within a language.

### Semantic models of time

- **Reichenbach (1947)** places every tense on three points: speech time (S), event time (E) and reference time (R). Past is E,R before S; past perfect is E before R before S. Bhat's deictic vs non-deictic split is a typologist's version of the S vs R distinction. Kannada's converbs ಮಾಡಿ / ಮಾಡುತ್ತ / ಮಾಡಲು locate E against another event, not against S.
- **Smith (1991)**, *The Parameter of Aspect*, gives aspect two components: situation type (lexical) and viewpoint (grammatical). Bhat takes this over directly.
- **Klein (1994)** replaces R with *topic time*, the time the utterance is about. Aspect then relates event time to topic time, and tense relates topic time to utterance time.

### Cross-linguistic typology

- **Comrie** (*Aspect* 1976, *Tense* 1985) defines tense as grammaticalised location in time and aspect as the internal temporal constituency of a situation. These are the definitions Bhat's are built on.
- **Dahl (1985)**, *Tense and Aspect Systems*, surveyed about 64 languages by questionnaire. He argues that TAM is better seen as a small universal set of *grams* (PFV, IPFV, PROG, PERFECT, FUTURE and so on) than as three tidy categories. Each language picks some and fuses them differently. On Dahl's view a language does not "choose tense"; it picks grams.
- **Bybee, Perkins and Pagliuca (1994)**, *The Evolution of Grammar*, sample 76 languages from 25 families. They find universal, one-way grammaticalisation paths: "have" or "finish" verbs become anteriors, then perfectives or pasts; "go" and "want" become futures. The book explicitly challenges generative grammar. It is the backdrop to Bhat's Chapter 7 point that aspect drifts toward tense over time.
- **WALS chapters 65–68 (Dahl and Velupillai)** map perfective/imperfective, past, future and perfect worldwide. They also note that tense and aspect often fuse in one form rather than staying separate categories.

### The Kannada grammatical tradition

- **Keśirāja, *Śabdamaṇidarpaṇa* (c. 1260)** is still the basis of standard school grammar. It describes Kannada through Sanskrit categories: kāla (tense) as past, present and future, plus vibhakti (cases) and liṅga (gender). Bhat's Book 14 argues that this misfits Old Kannada, which marks rational vs non-rational rather than three genders. The same logic applies to tense: on Bhat's reading Kannada morphology is past vs non-past, and the "future" is a use of the non-past.
- **Kittel (1903)** and the missionary grammars kept Keśirāja's framework and added English-style categories.
- **Modern learner grammars** often go further and give Kannada nine tenses: {past, present, future} × {simple, continuous, perfect}. This is the English tense-aspect grid, which is exactly the "red glass" Bhat warns about. On his account the "continuous" and "perfect" are optional periphrases (ಮಾಡುತ್ತಿದ್ದೆ, ಮಾಡಿದ್ದೆ), not tenses.

## Side by side

The frameworks differ most on one question: is TAM a fixed universal structure, or something each language builds differently?

| Framework | Unit of analysis | Universal or variable? | Kannada would be described as | What it predicts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bhat (1999) | Three categories, ranked within each language | Variable: each language makes one category prominent | Tense-prominent: obligatory past/non-past across finite and non-finite forms; aspect and mood optional | Correlated features: foregrounding by finite past, active verbs, tensed participles |
| Chomsky, GB (1981) | INFL node with ±Tense, AGR | Universal: every clause has INFL | Finite clauses have +Tense; converbs and participles are non-finite | Finiteness licenses nominative subjects |
| Pollock (1989) and Cinque (1999) | A fixed sequence of functional heads (Mood > T > Asp) | Universal, fixed order | Every head present; most are silent in Kannada | Suffix and adverb order follow the hierarchy |
| Ritter and Wiltschko (2009, 2014) | INFL as an anchoring slot with language-specific content | Universal slot, variable content | INFL = tense (like English) | Tenseless languages anchor by place or person instead |
| Reichenbach, Klein | Relations among S, R/topic time, E | Universal semantics | Converbs encode E relative to another event | A semantic space, not a typology |
| Comrie, Dahl, Bybee | Cross-linguistic grams and their history | Universal gram types, variable selection | A past gram, a non-past gram, and periphrastic progressive and perfect | One-way paths, e.g. completive to perfective to past |
| Keśirāja (c. 1260) | Sanskrit kāla categories | Sanskrit model applied | Three tenses: past, present, future | None stated; prescriptive |
| Learner grammars | English tense-aspect grid | English model applied | Nine tenses | None; pedagogical |

## Where they clash and where they meet

The real disagreement is about method and explanation, not about the Kannada facts. Most frameworks would accept Bhat's description of the Kannada verb. They differ on what makes that description true.

### Clashes

- **Universal skeleton vs real difference.** Cinque says every clause has the same roughly 30 TAM heads, mostly unpronounced. Bhat says a category that is optional and periphrastic in a language is not part of its grammar in the same way. Neither side has a decisive test, because silent heads cannot be directly observed.
- **Where explanation lives.** For Chomsky, TAM facts follow from an innate Universal Grammar. For Bybee and the typologists, they follow from usage and grammaticalisation. Bhat is closer to the typologists, but his prominence types are synchronic profiles, not histories.
- **Categories vs grams.** Dahl's gram model weakens Bhat's three-way split. If languages pick from a pool of grams that fuse tense and aspect, "tense-prominent" may mean only "picked a past gram and a non-past gram". Bhat's answer is pervasiveness: in Kannada, tense runs through the participles, converbs and verbal nouns, which is more than picking two grams.

### Points of contact

- **Ritter and Wiltschko vs Bhat.** Both say languages build clause anchoring on different categories. They differ on whether a universal slot sits underneath. A Ritter–Wiltschko reading of Bhat would be: Kannada fills INFL with tense, Ladakhi fills it with evidential mood.
- **Chomsky's tensedness as a scale.** Bhat's Chapter 7 does not reject the tensedness parameter. It turns it into a continuum and predicts that tense-prominent languages sit at the top.
- **Cinque's hierarchy vs Bhat's order of markers.** Bhat also tests the order of TAM markers in the verb. Kannada's stem + tense + person/number/gender order fits both accounts.

### Open questions

- Bhat's four criteria have no scoring procedure. A formal version, say a 0–3 score per criterion per category, would make the typology testable against WALS data.
- Is Hindi/Marathi really aspect-prominent? The claim rests on reading the "past" as perfective. Dahl and Velupillai's WALS data could be checked against it.
- Old Kannada vs modern Kannada: did aspect gain ground as vector verbs became more frequent? Bybee's one-way paths predict drift *toward* tense, not away from it.
- The repo's Book 32 summary is from a notes page. Chapters 5 to 7 should be read in full before any of this is published as Bhat's exact position.

## Case study: Japanese

Japanese is built much like Kannada and, like Kannada, has an obligatory two-way past/non-past. On Bhat's four criteria it still comes out as a weaker tense-prominent language than Kannada, and its history shows it reached tense by a different route. The shared structure is real but typological. It does not predict TAM prominence.

### Why "SOV" is the wrong starting point

Bhat objects to describing Kannada as SOV. The label imports the European idea that every clause has a grammatical subject and object. In Book 32 (Ch. 1) he says this assumption led earlier scholars to give Kannada and Manipuri European-style grammatical relations, and [Book 24](../dnsbhat/24-grammatical-relations/) argues that grammatical relations are neither necessary nor universal. The comparison below therefore lines up morphology and clause-building devices, not word-order slots.

Note for the repo: the `kannada-syntax` skill still opens with "SOV word order (strict)" as Kannada's first property. It should be reworded to follow Bhat.

### Structural parallels

| Device | Kannada | Japanese | Same or different |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Finite tense | ಮಾಡಿದನು / ಮಾಡುತ್ತಾನೆ (past / non-past) | tabe-ta / tabe-ru (past / non-past) | Same two-way split; no grammatical future in either |
| Person/number/gender agreement on the verb | Yes: ‑ಅನು, ‑ಅಳು, ‑ಅತು (rational vs non-rational) | None | Different |
| Tensed relative clause before the noun | ಮಾಡಿದ / ಮಾಡುವ ಹುಡುಗ | tabe-ta / tabe-ru hito | Same; both tense readings are relative to the main clause |
| Converbs: prior / simultaneous / purpose | ಮಾಡಿ / ಮಾಡುತ್ತ / ಮಾಡಲು | tabe-te / tabe-nagara / tabe-ni (iku) | Same three-way set |
| Converb shares a morpheme with the past | ಮಾಡಿ and ಮಾಡಿದ share ‑ಇ‑ | -te and -ta share -t- (-ta < -tari < -te ari) | Same pattern, different history |
| Conditional built on the past | ಮಾಡಿದ+ರೆ | tabe-ta+ra | Same |
| Concessive | ಮಾಡಿದರೂ | tabe-te mo | Same function |
| Progressive / resultative (converb + "be") | ಮಾಡುತ್ತಿದ್ದಾನೆ, ಬಂದಿದ್ದಾನೆ | tabe-te iru, ki-te iru | Same: optional periphrasis, not a tense |
| Vector verbs | ಮಾಡಿಬಿಡು (completive), ಮಾಡಿಕೊಡು (benefactive) | tabe-te shimau (completive), tabe-te ageru / kureru (benefactive) | Same |
| Modality by auxiliaries | ಮಾಡಬೇಕು, ಮಾಡಬಹುದು | tabe-nakereba naranai, tabe-te mo ii, taberu kamoshirenai | Same: outside the core paradigm |
| Verbal noun | ಮಾಡುವುದು / ಮಾಡಿದುದು | taberu koto / tabeta koto | Same, both tensed |
| Adjectives | Do not inflect; behave like nouns | i-adjectives inflect for tense like verbs (takai / takakatta) | Different |

### Bhat's four criteria, Japanese vs the others

Japanese passes on grammaticalisation and obligatoriness. It is weaker than Kannada on systematicity, and mixed on pervasiveness.

| Criterion | Kannada (tense-prominent) | Japanese | Hindi (Bhat: aspect-prominent) | Mandarin (aspect-prominent) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grammaticalisation of tense | Bound suffixes ‑ಇದ‑ / ‑ಉತ್ತ‑ | Bound suffixes -ta / -ru | "Past" is a perfective participle (*kiyaa*) | No tense morphology; aspect particles *le*, *guo*, *zhe* |
| Obligatoriness | Every finite verb | Every main-clause predicate, including adjectives and the copula | Perfective required in past contexts | Optional; time left to context |
| Systematicity | Closed paradigm crossed with person, number and gender (36 forms for ಮಾಡು) | Small paradigm, no agreement | Built around aspect, with tense on the auxiliary | Not applicable |
| Pervasiveness | Tense in participles, converbs and verbal nouns | Tense in relatives, verbal nouns and the -tara conditional | Participles carry aspect | Not applicable |

### Where Bhat's model fits Japanese

- **The two-way tense system.** It matches Kannada exactly, and the -ru form covers future time, as ಮುಂಬೊತ್ತು does.
- **Aspect and mood are periphrastic.** Japanese -te iru, -te shimau and modal auxiliaries sit outside the core paradigm, just as Kannada's vector verbs and ಬೇಕು / ಬಹುದು do.
- **History runs aspect to tense.** Late Old Japanese had a rich aspect and past system. -ta grew out of the resultative -tari and moved from aspect to tense, while -ki, -keri, -tu and -nu died out ([Narrog](https://benjamins.com/catalog/bct.123.05nar)). That is the drift Bhat discusses in Ch. 7, and Bybee's one-way path.
- **Mood used to be grammatical.** Old Japanese -keri marked hearsay, realisation and recognition of facts ([Suzuki, via PAJLS](https://journals.library.brandeis.edu/index.php/PAJLS/article/download/1680/1068)). Its contrast with -ki has been compared to the Turkish evidential pair -di / -miş ([Shinzato 1991](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/gengo1939/1991/99/1991_25/_article/-char/en)). So Old Japanese was closer to mood-prominent, and modern Japanese lost that.

### Where it does not fit

- **Adjectives.** Bhat predicts tense-prominent languages keep adjectives apart from verbs or group them with nouns. Japanese i-adjectives inflect for past exactly like negative verbs (atsui / atsukatta) ([Japanese grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_grammar)). Kannada fits the prediction; Japanese does not.
- **Foregrounding.** Bhat predicts the narrative mainline is carried by the finite past. Japanese narratives freely switch between -ta and -ru for past events ([Izumi](https://ejournal.undip.ac.id/index.php/izumi/article/downloadSuppFile/77411/21662)). Kannada narrative does use a historical present, but its mainline default is the past.
- **Stative verbs.** Bhat links tense-prominence with mostly active verbs. Japanese has a sizeable stative class (iru, aru, dekiru, wakaru). Kannada's ಇರು is also stative, but it is one verb with its own third tense.
- **Is -ta tense at all?** One school, following Kunihiro, treats -ta and -ru as perfective and imperfective markers ([Illinois thesis](https://ideals.illinois.edu/items/20102)). Most analysts treat main-clause -ta as tense but subordinate -ta as relative tense or aspect ([Takubo, Mito and Sawada](https://bun.kyoto-u.ac.jp/archive/jp/projects/projects_completed/hmn/langlogic/abstracts/Takubo&Mito&SawadaAbstract1.pdf)).

### Same structure, different prominence

Verb-final, agglutinative languages are spread across all three of Bhat's types. Clause structure does not predict which category a language makes prominent, which is Bhat's point against SOV-based typing.

| Language | Clause build | TAM backbone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kannada | Verb-final, agglutinative, converbs | Tense (Bhat) |
| Modern Japanese | Verb-final, agglutinative, converbs | Tense, weaker than Kannada |
| Late Old Japanese | Verb-final, agglutinative | Aspect and evidential past (-ki / -keri) |
| Turkish | Verb-final, agglutinative, converbs | Tense plus an evidential past (-di / -miş) |
| Hindi | Verb-final, partly analytic | Aspect (Bhat) |
| Ladakhi | Verb-final, agglutinative | Mood, with obligatory evidentials (Bhat) |

**Verdict.** Modern Japanese is tense-prominent by Bhat's criteria, but less strongly than Kannada. It fails two of his correlated features (adjectives, foregrounding), and its tense is younger, grown out of aspect. The Kannada–Japanese resemblance is in clause-building devices, which head-final agglutinative languages share whatever their TAM type.

*Open:* Bhat's own Ch. 6 may classify Japanese. That needs a check in the full book.

## Case study: Sanskrit

On Bhat's criteria, Vedic Sanskrit was aspect-prominent with a strong mood system, not tense-prominent. Classical Sanskrit lost most of those aspect distinctions, and its daughter Hindi rebuilt an aspect system that Bhat classes as aspect-prominent. So the Sanskrit frame that Keśirāja and later grammarians laid over Kannada came from a language whose TAM backbone was never Kannada's.

### What Bhat wrote about Sanskrit

Bhat's Sanskrit writing is mostly a critique of Sanskrit-based grammars *of Kannada*. He does not analyse Sanskrit TAM in its own right in the books collected so far.

- **[Book 14](../dnsbhat/14-nijakkU-haLegannaDa-vyAkaraNa-entahadu/)** (*ನಿಜಕ್ಕೂ ಹಳೆಗನ್ನಡ ವ್ಯಾಕರಣ ಎಂತಹದು?*) critiques *Śabdamaṇidarpaṇa* for applying Sanskrit categories to Old Kannada. Per the repo's notes on Ch. 9, the lakāra set was mapped onto Old Kannada verbs, although Old Kannada's tense markers are its own Dravidian forms. (Those notes name the forms; they have not been checked against the text.)
- **[Book 03](../dnsbhat/03-kannaDa-padagaLa-oLaracane/)** says Kannada has three independent base categories (noun, verb, adjective), unlike Sanskrit's primacy of the verb root (dhātu).
- **Books 01, 28 and 29** argue for a grammar built on Kannada's own structure. **Book 08** argues that the aspirated consonants serve no function in Kannada.
- **[Book 24](../dnsbhat/24-grammatical-relations/)** (*Grammatical Relations*) argues that grammatical relations are neither necessary nor universal. This is the deeper basis for his objection to "SOV".
- **Book 32** gives the closest TAM point: Hindi and Marathi are aspect-prominent because their "past" is a perfective. That perfective descends from the Sanskrit past participle in -ta.

### Sanskrit's TAM through time

| Stage | What carries the verb system | Bhat type (applying his criteria) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vedic | Aspect stems (present, aorist, perfect) carry the main split. Past is marked by the augment *a-*, which can be dropped when context makes the past clear. Rich mood: indicative, subjunctive, injunctive, optative, imperative. | Aspect-prominent, with mood close behind |
| Classical (Pāṇinian) | Ten lakāras for tense and mood. Aorist, imperfect and perfect have "fallen together" as interchangeable pasts. Subjunctive and injunctive gone; indicative, optative, imperative and conditional remain. | Weakly tense-based; aspect lost from the finite verb |
| Late Classical | Past increasingly carried by the participles -ta (kṛta) and -tavant | Aspect returning through participles |
| Middle Indic | One aspect-neutral finite past, later lost | Transitional |
| Hindi / Marathi | Past = perfective participle (kiyā); imperfective and progressive built on participles and "be" | Aspect-prominent (Bhat, Ch. 6) |

### Bhat's four criteria, Sanskrit vs Kannada

| Criterion | Vedic Sanskrit | Classical Sanskrit | Kannada |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Grammaticalisation | Aspect in the stem; tense only in a detachable augment | Tense and mood in lakāra endings | Tense in bound suffixes |
| Obligatoriness | Aspect stem required; augment optional | Lakāra required, but three "pasts" mean the same | Past/non-past required |
| Systematicity | Very high: person × number × voice × stem × mood | Very high, ten lakāras | High: 36 forms for ಮಾಡು |
| Pervasiveness | Participles by stem: present -ant, aorist, perfect -vas | Participles: present -ant, past -ta, future -syant | Tensed participles, converbs, verbal nouns |

### What this means for the Keśirāja critique

- **The import was mismatched twice.** Keśirāja brought in the kāla scheme (past, present, future) from Pāṇinian Sanskrit. That scheme already flattened Vedic's aspect system, and it suits Kannada's past/non-past even less.
- **Mood was put in the wrong place.** In Sanskrit, optative and imperative are lakāras within the core paradigm. In Kannada, obligation and possibility are periphrastic (ಮಾಡಬೇಕು, ಮಾಡಬಹುದು). A lakāra-shaped grammar treats them as Kannada verb forms on a par with past and non-past. Bhat's paradigm count keeps them out.
- **The participle story runs in opposite directions.** Sanskrit's -ta participle grew into an aspectual past in Hindi. Kannada's ಮಾಡಿದ / ಮಾಡುವ participles stayed *tensed*, which is part of Bhat's evidence for tense-prominence.
- **Classical Sanskrit is hard to type.** After the mid-1st millennium BCE it was learned as a second language of religion and scholarship. Bhat's obligatoriness criterion assumes a spoken norm, so Vedic is the fairer test case.

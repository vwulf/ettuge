## Diet, protein and longevity

**Plant-heavy diets with some animal food came out best for healthy aging, and the strictly plant-based index came out worst.**
Tessier et al. (*Nature Medicine*, 2025) scored eight dietary-pattern indices against a composite outcome: reaching 70 free of major chronic disease, with cognitive, physical and mental health intact. Every healthy pattern helped. The Alternative Healthy Eating Index (AHEI) was strongest, and the healthful plant-based index (hPDI) was weakest. The hPDI is the only index that gives *negative* scores to all animal foods, including fish and dairy. Popular summaries tend to skip that result, because it cuts against the "more plants is always better" reading. The data are self-reported food-frequency questionnaires from US health professionals, so the findings are associations, and the authors flag that the cohort may not generalise. The endpoint also builds physical function into the definition of healthy aging, so it already measures something close to frailty, but at an age when frailty is mostly not yet the limiting factor. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**The plant-to-animal protein benefit for heart disease is a substitution effect, and most of it comes from replacing red and processed meat.**
Glenn et al. (*AJCN*, 2024) followed about 200,000 people across the Nurses' Health Study, NHS II and the Health Professionals Follow-up Study for up to 30 years. The highest plant-to-animal protein ratio was associated with about 19% lower cardiovascular risk and about 27% lower coronary risk. Because the exposure is a ratio, the effect depends on what gets displaced. The benefit concentrates on red and processed meat, which is why dairy and fish can come out neutral or even positive in other analyses: swapping cheese for legumes is not the swap doing the work. Over the study period the cohort median moved only from about 1:3 to about 1:2. Even the "highest" group ate more animal than plant protein, so the observed reduction comes from a modest shift, not from anything like a low-protein or vegan diet. Mariotti's accompanying editorial on the ratio framing is still unread. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Animal protein tracks higher type 2 diabetes risk, but the headline 23% comes from a swap, not from cutting back.**
Malik et al. (*AJE*, 2016) used the same three cohorts: about 15,600 diabetes cases over 4.1 million person-years. Comparing the top and bottom fifths of intake, energy from animal protein was associated with about 13% higher risk, and vegetable protein with about 9% lower risk. Replacing 5% of energy from animal protein with vegetable protein was associated with about 23% lower risk. That last figure is often quoted as if it were the effect of eating less animal protein. It is the effect of a specific substitution. Several authors, Frank Hu and Vasanti Malik among them, appear on this paper, on Tessier and Glenn, and on the Fanti paper below. The four are best read as one research programme on shared data, not as four independent confirmations. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Adding methionine back to a low-protein longevity diet targets frailty, not lifespan.**
Fanti et al. (*Cell Metabolism*, 2026, from Valter Longo's lab) start from a puzzle: Southern European populations live long but have relatively high frailty rates. Aged (20-month-old), genetically diverse mice were fed standard, Western (high fat and sugar), ketogenic, or a low-protein, plant-based "longevity diet" with methionine added back (LDMM). Western and ketogenic diets raised fat mass and frailty and worsened either cholesterol or insulin resistance. LDMM lowered fat mass and frailty with no loss of lean mass and no calorie restriction. It lowered IGF-1 and raised growth hormone, GLP-1 and FGF21, and FGF21 was required for the fat-loss and insulin-sensitivity effects.

The key reading point: maximum lifespan did not differ between the longevity diet and control. Methionine restriction's lifespan benefit was already known in rodents. The open problem was that pushing restriction far enough wastes muscle. Lifespan and frailty respond to protein along different curves, and the supplement is tuned against the frailty curve while giving up as little of the lifespan benefit as possible. A single U-shaped "protein dial" is the wrong picture. It is a trade-off between two outcomes.

The human arm is cross-sectional: about 200,000 people, where the highest animal-protein group had healthier habits in other respects but roughly double the diabetes prevalence. That supports no causal claim. The authors disclose an equity stake in a medical-foods company, patents on the fasting-mimicking diet, and a provisional patent on this work. A human trial of LDMM is the stated next step. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Longo's *The Longevity Diet* is the prescriptive version of the same research.**
The book combines his lab work with fieldwork in long-lived regions such as Sardinia, Okinawa and Loma Linda. The core rules: mostly plants plus fish two or three times a week, and protein around 0.7–0.8 g/kg, mostly from legumes and nuts, raised again after about 65–70 when sarcopenia risk outweighs growth-signalling risk. Add generous olive oil and nuts, little sugar or refined grain, all eating within a 12-hour window, and periodic fasting-mimicking cycles. Read alongside Fanti, the book's later-life protein increase is the human-scale version of the methionine correction. Covered at summary level rather than read in full. **[→ health-fitness; cross-link → books-literature]**

---

**How much protein you need depends on which goal is in charge, and longevity and muscle gain pull in opposite directions.**
The useful anchors, in g per kg of body weight:

| Basis | Context |
| --- | --- |
| 0.8 g/kg | RDA: covers nearly everyone at maintenance |
| ~1.2 g/kg | active maintenance |
| ~1.6 g/kg | where muscle-gain benefit plateaus in resistance-training meta-analyses (Morton et al., 2018) |
| 2.3–3.1 g/kg of fat-free mass | for keeping lean mass during a calorie deficit (Helms et al.) |

For longevity or maintenance, the low end is plenty, and the Longo camp argues for keeping it low to hold IGF-1 and mTOR signalling down. For gaining lean mass in middle age, especially during a deficit, the evidence points toward the plateau. Plant proteins have lower leucine content and digestibility (DIAAS), so a mostly-plant eater may need roughly 10–20% more total protein for the same muscle-building effect. Both goals cannot be maximised at once. The practical compromise is a moderate range, mostly from plant sources, nearer the top on training days. The cohort papers above suggest the source matters more for long-term risk than the amount. One more practical point came up: protein targets worked out from formulas tend to drift above what a person actually eats, so start from a real food recall rather than from the formula. **[→ health-fitness]**

## Body composition and how it is measured

**Creatine raised lean mass and strength without exercise, but part of the lean-mass gain is probably water.**
Chun, Kreider et al. (*JISSN*, 2026) ran a 12-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in sedentary adults aged 45–65, with 64 completers. Participants took 10 g/day of creatine monohydrate or placebo, about the creatine in 2 kg of meat or fish a day. On top of that, participants *chose* whether to join a supervised exercise-plus-diet programme, giving four arms. Creatine raised DXA lean mass by about 1 kg even with no exercise, improved bench- and leg-press strength, and produced some exploratory memory gains.

Three things limit the headline. First, creatine draws water into muscle cells, and DXA counts that water as lean tissue. A 1 kg "lean" gain in 12 weeks without training fits water retention at least as well as new muscle protein, so the strength gains are the more convincing result. Second, the exercise arms were self-selected rather than randomised, so comparisons involving them are weaker than the creatine-versus-placebo comparison. Third, an earlier two-year randomised trial in about 200 older women found no lean-mass or strength benefit from creatine without resistance training. On context: the manufacturer supplied the creatine and funded one set of lab tests, and the senior author chairs its scientific advisory board. The paper says the company had no role in the analysis. The uncontested part of the creatine literature is still creatine *plus* resistance training. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**DXA visceral-fat readings mean something only against reference values, and those depend on the population, the device and the denominator.**
The Tromsø Study (*Journal of Obesity*, 2021) gives age- and sex-specific reference values for DXA-measured visceral adipose tissue (VAT) in adults over 40, with medians, percentiles and a metabolic-syndrome threshold for each band. Three reading notes came out of using it:
- **Use absolute measures.** The established metrics are VAT mass in grams, VAT area, the VAT-to-subcutaneous ratio, and waist circumference. "Visceral fat as a percentage of total body fat" is not a published metric. The well-known visceral *ratio*, around 60% in men, divides by *abdominal* fat, so the two percentages can't be compared.
- **Device and cohort matter.** The values come from one European cohort on one scanner platform. Commercial DXA services may use other hardware and algorithms, so a person's trend across repeat scans on the same machine is more informative than any single gram figure.
- **DXA visceral fat is itself an estimate.** CT and MRI are the gold standard.

Waist circumference is the cheap proxy to track between scans. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**At the same weight gain, South Asian men put more of it into visceral and ectopic fat and lost far more insulin sensitivity than white European men.**
In GlasVEGAS (McLaren et al., *Nature Metabolism*, 2024), matched South Asian and white European men were overfed to the same gain of about 4.5 kg. The European men put roughly three times more of the gain into lean tissue. Insulin sensitivity fell about 38% in the South Asian men versus about 7% in the Europeans, and the post-meal insulin rise was several times larger. The proposed mechanism is limited room to expand subcutaneous fat: South Asian adipocytes tend to be larger and less expandable, so once that buffer fills, excess energy spills over into the viscera, liver and muscle.

Two claims need to be kept apart:
- **The intercept:** at any given BMI or total fat, South Asians carry more visceral and ectopic fat and less muscle. This "thin-fat" phenotype is well established, and it is why guidelines set lower cut-offs for Asian populations. WHO Asian BMI cut-offs are 23 for overweight and 27.5 for obese, and the IDF central-obesity waist cut-off is 90 cm for South Asian men against 94 cm for Europid men.
- **The slope:** more of each *added* kilogram goes to visceral fat. GlasVEGAS and the mechanism support this, but clean ethnicity-specific coefficients are hard to find and vary by method.

The hopeful corollary is that visceral fat is usually mobilised first during fat loss, so the metabolic payoff per kilogram lost may be largest for the groups that carry the most of it. I read this paper through published write-ups, not in full. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**A low BMI is not the same as being lean, and a rough visceral-fat projection from repeat scans should be read as directional only.**
Two general modelling points came up while interpreting scans. First, a "lowest-ever weight" is not necessarily a lean-ever state. Someone light but with little muscle can carry a higher body-fat percentage than the scale suggests. A recomposition goal can then be "more muscle than ever before" rather than "go back to an earlier body." Second, visceral fat tracks total fat mass closely enough across a few scans to fit a line. Extrapolating that line far below the measured range ignores the floor of essential visceral fat, so the projection is a direction to aim in, not a number to hit, and the line should be refitted at each new scan. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Repeat DXA scans are only comparable when they are taken the same way.**
DXA lean mass is sensitive to hydration, and readings can swing by a kilogram or so from water alone. The standard advice is to match conditions across scans: same time of day, fasted, similarly hydrated, and no hard training just before. Lean mass changes slowly, so scanning about once a year is enough. More often mostly adds noise. The same water caveat explains the creatine result above. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**VO₂max is reported per kilogram, so weight change alone can move it.**
A falling VO₂max estimate is not necessarily lost fitness. VO₂max in ml/kg/min has body mass in the denominator, so gaining weight with the same heart-and-lung "engine" lowers the number proportionally, and losing fat raises it with no change in the engine. Before treating a decline as detraining, check whether the absolute value (litres per minute) changed, or adjust for the weight difference. **[→ health-fitness]**

## Sitting, squatting and active rest

**Leg-muscle activity fades within minutes of sitting down, and about 25 minutes may be a tipping point.**
Brakenridge et al. (*npj Exercise Medicine and Health*, 2026) recorded thigh-muscle activity over 7,684 real-world sitting bouts in 84 adults, using textile EMG sensors built into shorts. During continuous sitting, activity decays roughly as a power law: it is detectable about 10% of the time by ~9 minutes and about 1% by ~26 minutes. People whose usual bouts were under 25 minutes had about 10.4 minutes of muscle activity per hour of sitting, against about 6.6 for those whose bouts ran 25 minutes or more, roughly 50% more. Frequent, vigorous breaks added about five more minutes per hour, and sitting after real activity kept the muscles more active than sitting after standing.

This is the "sedentary time is a separate risk from exercise" story: someone can complete every workout and still spend most of a desk day with near-silent leg muscles. Muscle inactivity is a proposed driver of insulin resistance and visceral fat. The caveats are that the study is small and observational, and that "break every 25 minutes" is the authors' extrapolation, not a tested intervention. Even so, it is cheap and plausibly useful. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**How you rest matters: squatting and kneeling keep the leg muscles working, and a chair does not.**
Raichlen et al. (*PNAS*, 2020) found that Hadza hunter-gatherers spend about as many hours a day resting as office workers, around ten, but mostly squatting, kneeling or sitting on the ground, and they have very low rates of cardiometabolic disease. A chair takes the body's weight and lets the postural muscles (quads, glutes, calves) go quiet. A flat-footed squat means supporting and balancing your own mass, so those muscles stay lightly engaged throughout. The authors' "inactivity mismatch" hypothesis says the harm comes from prolonged muscular *inactivity*, not from rest as such, which is the mechanism Brakenridge measures directly.

Practically, mixing squatting, kneeling and floor-sitting into rest and desk time adds "active rest," and it doubles as practice for the ankle, hip and knee mobility that deep-squat positions (such as the cossack squat) need. Two limits. Raichlen measured postures and inferred the benefit, and nobody has yet shown that squat-resting lowers visceral fat. And deep loaded knee flexion can aggravate existing knee problems, so tolerance should be built gradually. **[→ health-fitness; cross-link → martial-arts]**

## Lactate and the heart

**The heart burns lactate inside its mitochondria, and that lets it burn glucose and lactate at the same time.**
Cluntun et al. (*Nature*, 2026) show that in mouse heart muscle the monocarboxylate transporter MCT1 sits on the mitochondria and imports lactate, which LDHB then oxidises to pyruvate and NADH. Glucose is processed in the cytosol and lactate in the mitochondria, so the two pathways run side by side. In most tissues they compete: using more of one suppresses the other. Isolated heart cells preferred lactate when both fuels were available. The accompanying News & Views notes that this runs against long-standing doubt that lactate enters mitochondria directly. It is strong support, at least in heart muscle, for George Brooks' intracellular lactate-shuttle hypothesis from exercise physiology. **[→ mathematics-science]**

---

**The paper shows the pathway is *necessary* under disease stress. It does not show that more lactate, or more exercise, makes the heart stronger.**
Every functional result comes from *removing* MCT1, by knockout or with the inhibitors AZD3965 and 7ACC2. Knockout hearts could not adapt to pressure overload or neurohormonal stress, and after ischaemia–reperfusion they had larger damaged areas and never recovered function, while control hearts returned to baseline within about seven weeks. Stressed and failing hearts, including human samples, showed *more* mitochondrial MCT1, which suggests an adaptive response. But at baseline, knockout mice had normal heart function at one year and unchanged maximal treadmill capacity at 4–12 weeks, because the mitochondrial pyruvate carrier covers for MCT1 when nothing is wrong. The treadmill protocol was also designed *not* to train the mice.

So there is no sufficiency test: no MCT1 overexpression, no lactate supplementation, no training intervention. "Higher lactate exposure protects the heart" remains untested. Even if the pathway is essential, feeding it more need not help, because the knockouts were limited by transport, not supply, and resting blood lactate already supplies the pathway continuously. Other limits: sample sizes were often 3–10, some tracing was done under anaesthesia (which distorts lactate turnover), and the in-vivo data can't cleanly separate MCT1's mitochondrial role from its role at the cell membrane. **[→ mathematics-science; cross-link → health-fitness]**

---

**This paper does not settle whether training across heart-rate zones is worthwhile. That case rests on older evidence.**
The question was whether the paper supports training across intensity zones: whether varied-intensity aerobic work exercises different metabolic pathways in skeletal muscle, and whether the lactate from hard efforts "keeps in shape" the heart's dual-fuel system. It does not directly support either. The heart's parallel pathways are the *exception*, so the finding does not transfer to skeletal muscle. Whether skeletal-muscle mitochondria import lactate directly is still debated. The established reasons to train across zones are older: easy volume builds mitochondrial density, capillaries and fat oxidation, and threshold and interval work raise lactate production and clearance. Endurance training is known to increase MCT1 in skeletal muscle. It is also well established that the heart's lactate uptake rises with blood lactate during hard efforts, so intervals do send the heart more lactate. What is missing is any evidence that this *trains* the pathway. The experiment that would answer it would train animals or people at different intensities and measure cardiac mitochondrial MCT1 and lactate oxidation. The paper itself points to Zhang et al. (*Science Advances*, 2024), on MCT1-transported lactate promoting mitochondrial biogenesis in skeletal muscle, as the more relevant evidence for lactate as a training signal. That one is still unread. **[→ health-fitness]**

## Training intensity: zones, thresholds and tests

**"Zone 2" means three different things depending on who is talking.**
- **On a watch, or to a running coach,** using a five-zone model built on percentages of max heart rate, Zone 2 is easy, conversational aerobic running.
- **In the metabolic sense** popularised in longevity circles (Iñigo San Millán, Peter Attia), Zone 2 is the highest intensity you can hold below your *first* ventilatory or lactate threshold (VT1/LT1): fat oxidation is high and lactate is stable. That depends on your own physiology, not on a fixed percentage of max.
- **In the three-zone polarized model** used in endurance research, "Zone 2" is the grey zone *between* the two thresholds, which polarized training tries to minimise.

The same words can mean opposite things. When in doubt, ignore the label and ask which threshold the effort sits relative to. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Zones based on percentage of max heart rate can sit well below a lab-measured threshold, so threshold-anchored zones are more reliable.**
A lab test with gas exchange finds the two ventilatory thresholds (VT1 and VT2) directly, and they split effort into three physiological domains: moderate, heavy and severe. Default watch zones instead assume thresholds sit at fixed percentages of max HR (aerobic threshold around 70%). In someone whose thresholds sit high relative to max, metabolic Zone 2 lands in the *watch's* Zone 3. Obeying the watch then leaves aerobic work undone, and the watch's Zone 3 hides the crossover into the heavy domain. The fix is custom zones set in beats per minute from the lab thresholds, or zones based on lactate-threshold heart rate (LTHR) that line up with them. Two further cautions:
- **Watch lactate-threshold estimates** approximate LT2, the second threshold. They should be compared with the lab's VT2, not VT1, and they can overestimate it noticeably. A threshold normally sits around 85–90% of true max HR, which is a quick sanity check.
- **Max HR from a single lab test** may be below what you can reach in a hard race or hill effort. If field efforts exceed it, the zones should be rebuilt from the higher figure.

**[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Respiratory exchange ratio (RER) tells you whether a VO₂max test was truly maximal.**
RER is exhaled CO₂ divided by inhaled O₂ (VCO₂/VO₂), and it comes free with any gas-exchange test. Burning pure fat gives about 0.70 and pure carbohydrate about 1.00, so RER climbs from around 0.8 at easy efforts toward 1.0 as intensity rises. Above that, buffering lactate releases *extra* CO₂ unrelated to fuel, which pushes RER past 1.0. So peak RER is objective evidence of effort. Above about 1.15 means a genuine maximal test. About 1.10–1.15 is solid. Below about 1.05 suggests the test stopped early, often because the subject was unused to the treadmill, and that the measured VO₂max underestimates the true ceiling. The stage-by-stage RER curve is also one of the ways labs cross-check where VT1 and VT2 fall, so the full test report is worth asking for. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Consumer watch VO₂max estimates are biased by how and where you run.**
Watch VO₂max estimates come from the relation between pace and heart rate on outdoor GPS runs with sustained elevated heart rate. They update only from those runs, they favour flat terrain (hill efforts are under-credited, because pace drops while heart rate stays high), and threshold detection usually needs a chest strap. Erg, bike and indoor sessions don't feed the estimate at all. The estimate can sit several points above a lab value, so it is better used as a trend than as an absolute number. Activity labelling matters too: sessions filed under the wrong sport (a ski erg logged as indoor rowing, for example) contaminate the history for both sports. A custom activity profile per machine keeps the records clean. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**For long-term outcomes, building fitness up from a low base pays off more than refining a high one.**
Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality, and the relationship is steepest at the bottom: the largest risk reductions come from moving out of the lowest fitness categories, not from going from good to elite. One popular source puts a health target around 14 METs (VO₂max ≈ 50 ml/kg/min). That is a reasonable anchor rather than a hard threshold, and it makes VO₂max work a high-value priority for anyone starting from the low end. A weekly target of roughly 60 MET-hours is a commonly cited volume associated with large mortality reductions. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**For disease risk, the benefit keeps growing until about 3,000–4,000 MET-minutes a week, roughly 900 minutes of brisk walking or its equivalent. That is several times the guideline minimum.**
Kyu et al. (*BMJ*, 2016) ran a dose-response meta-analysis for the Global Burden of Disease study. It pooled 174 prospective cohort studies of *total* physical activity (leisure, work, transport and household) against breast cancer, colon cancer, diabetes, ischaemic heart disease and ischaemic stroke. The guideline minimum is 600 MET-minutes a week, about 150 minutes of brisk walking (≈4 METs) or 75 minutes of running. Risk kept falling well past that. Most of the gain came by 3,000–4,000 MET-minutes a week, and returns diminished beyond. For diabetes, going from 600 to 3,600 MET-minutes a week cut risk by a further 19%, but going from 9,000 to 12,000 cut it by only 0.6%. Compared with insufficiently active people (<600), the highly active (≥8,000) had 14–28% lower risk across the five diseases.

At 4 METs, 3,600 MET-minutes is 900 minutes a week, which is the origin of a 900-minute weekly target. Watch "intensity minutes" are close to MET-weighted, because vigorous minutes count double, so they roughly track the same quantity. Two caveats. Kyu counts activity in every domain, not just exercise, so 900 minutes of workouts alone is more than the curve needs. And the curve comes from self-reported, observational data.

The mortality picture is flatter. Arem et al. (*JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2015) pooled about 661,000 adults and found 31% lower mortality at one to two times the leisure-time guideline. Mortality benefit topped out at 39% lower around three to five times the guideline (roughly 450–750 moderate minutes a week), with no harm even at ten times. So most of the *survival* benefit arrives early, while *disease-specific* risk keeps improving at higher volumes. The two papers are complementary. Neither shows that the extra volume itself is causal, and the most active people differ in other ways too. **[→ health-fitness]**

## Interval formats and cross-training

**The Norwegian 4×4 and short hill sprints sit at opposite ends of the interval spectrum.**

| | Norwegian 4×4 | 10–15 s hill sprints | SIT / Wingate-style (~20–30 s) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rep length | 4 min | 10–15 s | 20–30 s all-out |
| Intensity | ~90–95% max HR | near-maximal effort, too short for HR to peak | maximal |
| Recovery | 3 min easy jog: active and deliberately incomplete | 2–3 min walk: full | ~2 min easy: full |
| Main system | central aerobic: stroke volume, cardiac output, VO₂max | neuromuscular: fibre recruitment, tendon stiffness, running economy | glycolytic, plus a fast mitochondrial signal |

The 4×4 works because four minutes is long enough to drive you up to VO₂max and hold you there, and the short recoveries keep you near it. Sprints want full recovery so every rep is powerful rather than a lactate grind. They are a power and economy supplement that also preserves fast-twitch fibres that fade with age, not a substitute for the 4×4. The two should not go on the same day or back-to-back days. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**In a 4×4, "90–95% of max HR" is where you end up, not the pace you start at.**
Heart rate lags effort, so the first minute of each rep reads low. Hold a hard pace you can repeat, not an all-out sprint, and let heart rate climb into the target band by the end of each rep. Warm up for 10–15 minutes first. Checking the distance covered in each rep is a good honesty test: equal distances mean good pacing, while a steady fade across the four reps means the first was too fast. The fade tells you something useful, and the remedy is to start more conservatively, not to push harder. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**REHIT (two or three 20-second all-out sprints) is a time-efficient, zero-impact VO₂max session, as long as it really is all-out.**
On a bike or bike erg the protocol is about ten minutes: an easy warm-up, then 20 s all-out, 2 min easy, repeated two or three times, then a cool-down. The effort *is* the stimulus. Most people hold back on a truly maximal 20 seconds and get little from it. The full two-minute recoveries matter, because cutting them drops each sprint's intensity and blunts the adaptation. It is best used *instead of* another VO₂max session, for example as a make-up for a missed week, never on top of one, and kept at least 48 hours from other maximal work. It does not replace easy aerobic volume or strength work. Per hour, sprint-interval training gives the biggest mitochondrial and VO₂max return, but it does not raise capillary density, which is the job of endurance volume (see the Mølmen meta-regression below).

On a smart trainer, power is the *feedback*, not the target. A genuine all-out 20 s typically averages several times a rider's easy endurance power. **SIM or free-ride mode** (fixed resistance, uncapped power) suits all-out sprints better than **ERG mode**, which holds a set wattage and so caps the effort. Using one fixed gear avoids mid-sprint shifts and dropped chains. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**To substitute an erg session for a run, match time and heart rate, not distance.**
Distance on ergs is only loosely comparable to running distance. On water-tank rowers it isn't reliable at all, because the readout depends on the water level. The robust way to replace a run is to match duration and average heart rate, or power on machines that measure it well, such as air-resistance ski and rowing ergs and smart trainers. Substitutes replace the aerobic dose, but not the impact loading of running: tendon, cartilage and neuromuscular adaptation. So keep running days for running, and substitute only on the days you can't run. A long erg session also needs its own build-up, because jumping from short sessions straight to an hour or more risks shoulder or lat strain. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Best efforts on several machines form one power-duration curve, which gives you a critical power estimate.**
An athlete's best sustainable power falls predictably as duration rises, typically by around 8–10% each time the duration doubles in the few-minutes-to-half-hour range. So best efforts at different durations on *different* calibrated machines (a ski erg, a rowing erg and a smart trainer) should line up on one curve, and when they do they give a solid estimate of critical power, the boundary between the heavy and severe domains. Above about 45 minutes, the three machines converge on similar wattages, so the choice can follow what the joints tolerate that day. Standard formulas (for example the ACSM cycling equation, VO₂ ≈ 10.8 × watts/kg + 7) can turn submaximal power into a VO₂max estimate. They work only if the efforts really were near-maximal, which a heart-rate trace or a dedicated 20-minute test can confirm. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Ergs and swimming beat resistance training for VO₂max and fat loss, but they can't do its job for muscle and bone.**

| Modality | Muscle | Bone | VO₂max | Fat loss | Knee-friendliness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Resistance training | High | High | Low | Medium | Medium |
| Rowing erg | Low–medium | Low | High | High | High |
| Ski erg | Low | Low | High | High | Highest |
| Swimming | — | None | Low–medium | Low–medium | Highest |

Hypertrophy needs progressive overload, meaning heavier loads near failure, added over time. Ergs and swimming can go *harder* but not *heavier*, so in a detrained person they add only a first slice of muscle and then plateau. Swimming is non-weight-bearing and does essentially nothing for bone density: competitive swimmers have lower bone density than other athletes. The rowing erg is among the best VO₂max builders and is pull-dominant, so it touches the upper back more than running does. The ski erg is nearly as good aerobically and the gentlest land option for knees. A useful split: ergs for the aerobic side and the fat-loss denominator, and loaded strength work for the lean-mass and bone numerator. Whether resistance work becomes muscle also depends on eating enough protein, especially in a calorie deficit. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Loaded carries build strength only when they are heavy and short.**
A long walk carrying a light kettlebell (under ~10% of body weight, over a mile or two) is a pleasant loaded walk. It is easy aerobic work with some grip and posture, but too light and too long to load trunk muscle or bone. Carries do strength work when treated like lifting sets: heavy load, about 20–40 m per trip, rest between. The **suitcase carry** (one heavy bell on one side) loads the obliques and deep trunk against side-bending. Rack and double carries load the traps, upper back and grip. It is the same tool at the opposite dose. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Every intensity grows mitochondria about equally. Sprints do it fastest per hour, and only endurance and high-intensity training raise capillary density.**
Mølmen, Almquist and Skattebo (*Sports Medicine*, 2025) meta-regressed training studies covering nearly 6,000 participants, comparing endurance training (ET), high-intensity intervals (HIT) and sprint intervals (SIT).
- **Mitochondria:** content rose by similar amounts in all three: about 23% for ET and about 27% for HIT and SIT. The rise showed up within two weeks and continued for up to 25 weeks with ET. Per hour of training, SIT was about 2.3 times as effective as HIT and 3.9 times as effective as ET. In well-trained people, only SIT still gave significant per-hour gains.
- **Capillaries:** capillaries *per fibre* rose similarly across all three. Capillary *density* rose only with ET and HIT, with ET giving 5–10% more than the others. Untrained and moderately trained people gained about 10–20%, the gains came within about four weeks and then plateaued, and well-trained people showed no significant change.
- **VO₂max:** it rose 9–12% with every modality, statistically indistinguishable. Per hour, SIT was about 2.9 times as effective as HIT and about 5 times as effective as ET. Six sessions a week beat four, and four beat two.
- **Predictors:** baseline fitness was the strongest predictor of how much people adapted. Age, sex and disease status made no significant difference to the mitochondrial response, so the machinery stays trainable into later life.

The usual explanation for the capillary difference is shear stress. Sustained blood flow during long moderate efforts is the stimulus for growing new capillaries. Brief maximal efforts send a strong mitochondrial signal (AMPK, PGC-1α) but don't keep up that flow. This is the reviewers' and commentators' interpretation, not something the meta-regression tests. The practical upshot is that the modalities complement each other. Sprints are the time-efficient lever for mitochondria and VO₂max, and continuous easy volume builds the delivery network that lets those mitochondria sustain long efforts. That is the physiology behind the 80/20 split below and Attia's Zone 2 base plus one hard session. Caveats: the protocols and populations are heterogeneous, and per-hour efficiency is not the same as total adaptation for someone who has the time to do the volume. Read via a summary write-up, not the full paper. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Most cardio should be easy, with a little very hard work and not much in between.**
Across the endurance literature, roughly 80% of training time below the first threshold and roughly 20% at high intensity works better than spending most sessions in the moderately hard grey zone. Easy volume builds capillaries and mitochondria and is the sustainable lever for weight. Intervals build VO₂max. Strength work preserves the muscle all of it runs on. Consistency at a sustainable weekly volume matters more than the exact numbers. Weight in particular tends to follow sustained training load over months rather than any single block. **[→ health-fitness]**

## Popular sources checked against the papers

**"What Harvard & USC Just Learned About Protein" (YouTube) summarises the four protein papers fairly, but blurs their evidence levels.**
The video gets the main results right. What it misses:
- It presents Fanti's mouse and cross-sectional data alongside the prospective cohort papers as if they carried equal weight.
- It folds cross-sectional diabetes prevalence into prospective risk.
- It quotes substitution effects as if they were intake effects.
- It skips Tessier's weak hPDI result, the FGF21/GLP-1 mechanism, and the authors' commercial disclosures.

The format is an author explaining her own paper to a sympathetic host, which is fine as explanation but is not a check on the work. The video does correctly describe the methionine "Goldilocks zone" as a trade-off between lifespan and frailty. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Christopher Gardner (Stanford) on ZOE makes the strongest version of "protein is overhyped." It is right for most people, but not for someone deliberately building muscle.**
His points hold for the general population:
- 0.8 g/kg covers about 97.5% of people, and most already eat well above it.
- Plant protein is complete in aggregate, so the "incomplete protein" idea is a myth.
- Bars and shakes are mostly marketing, and whole-food legumes do better.
- His trial comparing vegan and omnivore athletes found identical performance.

His muscle arithmetic works out the protein actually laid down in a year's muscle gain at only a few grams a day. That assumes enough total energy and ignores the higher needs for keeping lean mass during a calorie deficit and the lower digestibility of plant protein. So the argument does not extend to middle-aged recomposition, where the evidence still points toward the ~1.6 g/kg plateau. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**A longevity-coaching video has a sound framework, but its specific prescriptions reflect the presenter's own situation.**
"Fix the weakest area, not the strongest" and "measure outcomes, not activities" are good principles. Its advice to cut protein, and to rank VO₂max above strength, makes sense for someone already very muscular and strong. For someone starting from the opposite profile, the same logic gives the opposite prescription. Its rodent methionine-restriction evidence is real, but the human evidence is thin and mostly about insulin sensitivity rather than lifespan. Its testimonials ("biomarker X halved in five weeks", "top 1% across 100 biomarkers") are marketing for a paid community. The parts worth keeping are well supported and unexciting: fibre around 40–50 g/day, consistent sleep timing (within about ±30 minutes), and attention to visceral fat. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**A VO₂max-training video's case for sprint intervals holds up, with the usual caveats.**
Its claims checked out: short all-out sprint protocols raise VO₂max efficiently, cutting the recovery periods undermines them, and cardiorespiratory fitness strongly predicts mortality. What it underplays: the effort must be genuinely maximal, the protocol doesn't replace easy volume, and stacking it on top of other hard sessions breaks recovery. See the REHIT entry in [Interval formats and cross-training](ch7). **[→ health-fitness]**

## *Outlive* against the papers

Peter Attia's *Outlive* (2023, with Bill Gifford) covers much of the same ground as the papers above. Each entry below takes one of its main claims and sets it beside the evidence in the earlier chapters. The book's own summary and a reader's ten takeaways are in [Books › Outlive](https://vwulf.github.io/ettuge/Books/influential/outlive).

---

**Medicine 3.0: act on risk decades before disease, because all four "horsemen" start early.**
Attia's framework targets atherosclerotic heart disease, cancer, neurodegeneration and metabolic dysfunction. All four develop over decades, so waiting for a diagnosis is waiting too long. The approach is to measure early and often (apoB, Lp(a), oral glucose tolerance with insulin, liver fat, body composition, VO₂max), then act on the trend. This fits the reading habits in this page: trust the trend in your own repeat measurements over any single reading, and treat population cut-offs as starting points that depend on who you are. Two cautions run the other way. Much of the book's case for acting earlier rests on extrapolating from observational data and mechanism rather than on trials of early intervention, which Attia sometimes concedes. And testing heavily also produces false alarms and incidental findings. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**VO₂max is the strongest single predictor of all-cause mortality, so aim to be elite for your age, then for two decades younger.**
The book's anchor is a large treadmill cohort study (Mandsager et al., 2018), in which the least fit had several times the mortality of the most fit. The gap was larger than for smoking or diabetes. Attia's target is the top 2% for your age and sex and then for people 20 years younger, because VO₂max falls roughly 10% a decade and he wants a reserve that still leaves you capable in your eighties. This matches the [training-intensity notes](ch6): the steepest gains come from moving off the bottom, and VO₂max is per kilogram, so fat loss counts as well as fitness. The caveat is that the data are observational. Fit people differ from unfit people in many ways, and no trial has randomised people to high VO₂max. Even so, the dose–response pattern, and the fact that training raises VO₂max, make this one of the book's best-supported claims. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Zone 2 is the metabolic definition, at about three hours a week, and it builds the machinery for burning fat.**
Attia adopts Iñigo San Millán's definition: the highest effort you can hold with lactate stable, just below the first threshold. The test he gives is that you can talk, but would rather not. His minimum is about three hours a week, for example four 45-minute sessions, and he does more himself. The upper reference point is professional cyclists, who train for well over 20 hours a week (up to roughly 35 at peak), mostly at this intensity. San Millán and Brooks (*Sports Medicine*, 2018) compared professional cyclists, moderately active men and sedentary men with metabolic syndrome. The professionals kept blood lactate low and fat oxidation high even at high power, while the sedentary group had higher lactate *at rest* than the professionals did while working hard. Pro-level volume isn't realistic for anyone else. The point is direction: the further along the volume curve, the closer the metabolism gets to the fat-burning profile that people with diabetes lack. The rationale is mitochondrial: easy volume builds mitochondrial density and fat oxidation, keeping a person "metabolically flexible" rather than reliant on glucose. This is the metabolic meaning of "Zone 2" in [Training intensity](ch6), and it is why lab thresholds beat watch percentages for setting it. Two limits. First, the mitochondrial argument for Zone 2 *specifically*, as opposed to easy aerobic volume generally, rests largely on athlete data and mechanism. Second, the [heart-lactate paper](ch5) shows how far mechanism can run ahead of training evidence. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**One weekly VO₂max session of four-minute intervals is enough to build the peak.**
The book's VO₂max session is four to six four-minute intervals, each at the hardest pace you can hold steadily for four minutes, with four minutes of easy recovery. That is essentially the Norwegian 4×4, done about once a week on top of the Zone 2 base. This is the roughly 80/20 split in [Interval formats and cross-training](ch7). The pacing advice there applies too: heart rate gets to 90–95% of max by the *end* of a rep, and a fade across reps means the first one was too fast. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Strength is a longevity tool, with grip, pulling, hip-hinging and eccentric control as its core.**
Attia argues that muscle and strength decline steeply with age and inactivity. Falls are the leading cause of injury death in adults over 65 (CDC), and a hip fracture in old age is often the start of a steep decline. His centenarian-decathlon logic works backwards: to carry groceries, climb stairs or lift a grandchild at 90, you need a large reserve at 40 or 50, because strength will fall from wherever it starts. He emphasises grip (dead hangs, carries), pulling, hip-hinging, and controlling the lowering phase of movements, especially going downhill or downstairs. This matches the [ergs-versus-resistance-training table](ch7): ergs and swimming can't replace loaded strength work for muscle and bone, and carries do strength work only when they are heavy and short. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Stability is the most neglected pillar.**
The fourth pillar is stability: controlling force through joints with good posture, breathing and foot and trunk mechanics. The book draws on Dynamic Neuromuscular Stabilization (DNS), a rehabilitation approach that retrains movement patterns from infant development. Attia treats poor stability as the hidden cause of many injuries in otherwise strong, fit people, and as something best learned with a coach because it is hard to see in yourself. This links to [active rest](ch4): squatting and kneeling postures are both "active rest" and stability practice, and both need building up gradually around problem joints. The evidence base for DNS specifically is thinner than for strength or aerobic training. **[→ health-fitness; cross-link → martial-arts]**

---

**Nutrition has three levers (how much, what and when), and protein matters more than any diet label.**
Attia's nutrition chapters are deliberately non-dogmatic. Energy balance comes first, and food choice and meal timing are tools for getting it right, not ends in themselves. He has moved away from ketogenic eating and long fasts, largely because of concern about losing muscle. His firmest position is protein: at least about 1.6 g/kg a day, spread across meals, more than most people eat. Alcohol gets little defence.

Set against the papers, this is one pole of the [protein tension](ch2). Attia wants muscle and frailty protection now, while Longo and Fanti want low protein for lifespan signalling, with methionine added back only to prevent frailty. The cohort papers add a third point that the calorie-first framing leaves out: for heart disease and diabetes, protein *source* matters at the same total intake, and most of the benefit comes from replacing red and processed meat with plant protein. A practical synthesis is enough protein for muscle, mostly from plants, inside a calorie budget. Strict versions of either camp go further than the evidence. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**ApoB is the cholesterol number to track, and lowering it early beats lowering it late.**
The book treats apoB, the protein on every atherogenic particle (LDL, VLDL, IDL and Lp(a)), as the causal driver of atherosclerosis, and lifetime exposure as what matters. Hence his aggressive, early targets, below mainstream guideline thresholds, using statins, ezetimibe or PCSK9 inhibitors as needed. Lp(a) is largely genetic and worth measuring once. Dietary cholesterol has a small effect on blood levels for most people, so the levers are saturated fat, weight and drugs, and raising HDL has not lowered risk in trials. The causal role of apoB is among the best-established findings in cardiology, and a 2022 European consensus statement endorses apoB as a primary lipid target. The specific very-low targets for primary prevention go beyond current trial evidence and should be discussed with a physician. For South Asians, the lower cut-offs in [Body composition](ch3) and the higher average Lp(a) point the same way: work out individual risk early, from family history and an Lp(a) test, rather than assuming average risk. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Metabolic health: catch insulin resistance years before glucose rises, and don't expect metformin to do exercise's job.**
Attia argues that insulin resistance and liver fat appear long before fasting glucose or HbA1c become abnormal, so he uses oral glucose tolerance tests with insulin, and sometimes continuous glucose monitors. He treats HbA1c as useful but imprecise, because it depends on how long red blood cells live. On metformin he is lukewarm. He took it for years, then stopped, citing evidence that it can blunt the gains from training. The randomised trial of metformin for aging (TAME) has not yet reported. That is why "exercise first, drugs as a backstop" is the safer reading. This fits [GlasVEGAS](ch3) and the [sitting notes](ch4): visceral fat and muscle inactivity are among the earliest drivers of insulin resistance, and training and fat loss are the levers. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Sleep and emotional health are pillars in their own right.**
The book gives sleep its own chapter: roughly seven to nine hours, regular timing, and treating sleep problems as medical problems, since poor sleep worsens insulin sensitivity, appetite, training recovery and dementia risk. The emotional-health chapter is the book's most personal. Attia describes his own long struggle and the therapy that helped, and argues that living longer is pointless if those years are unhappy. Neither topic has papers in this collection, but both connect to it. Consistent sleep timing was one of the well-supported parts of the [longevity video](ch8), and recovery is the limit that rules out stacking hard sessions. **[→ health-fitness]**

---

**Where *Outlive* is weakest.**
- **Evidence:** much of the book extrapolates from observational cohorts, mechanism and Attia's own practice, a point reviewers made. The strongest claims (VO₂max, strength, apoB) are also the best supported. The weaker ones are specific drug and supplement choices.
- **Cost:** the testing-heavy approach is expensive and assumes access to a willing physician.
- **Interpretation:** a calorie-first summary can lose the cohort-paper finding that food *source* matters independently of weight.
- **Author:** in February 2026 Attia left his CBS News contributor role and a corporate science role after released documents showed correspondence with Jeffrey Epstein. He apologised and denied any criminal involvement. This bears on the author's judgement, not on the evidence the book cites, which should be judged on its own terms.

## Reading habits that kept coming up

1. **Necessity is not sufficiency.** "Removing X hurts" doesn't mean "adding X helps" (Cluntun).
2. **A substitution result is not a main effect.** Always ask what replaced what (Glenn, Malik).
3. **Check for shared cohorts and authors.** Several "independent" findings come from the same NHS/HPFS data and the same research group.
4. **Weigh the study design.** Mouse, cross-sectional, prospective and randomised evidence carry different weight, and popular summaries often mix them (Fanti and the protein video).
5. **Know what the instrument measures.** DXA lean mass includes water (creatine), DXA visceral fat is a device-dependent estimate (Tromsø), and watch VO₂max is pace-based and terrain-biased.
6. **Check the denominator.** VO₂max per kilogram moves with weight, and visceral-fat ratios divide by abdominal fat, not total fat.
7. **Look for what got left out.** Tessier's weakest result was the one the summaries skipped.
8. **Read the disclosures, but don't stop there.** Funding and patents are context, not refutation (Fanti, the creatine trial).
9. **Separate the intercept from the slope.** "Higher at baseline" and "rises faster" are different claims (GlasVEGAS).
10. **Labels drift.** "Zone 2" names three different things. Anchor to the physiology instead.
11. **Don't extrapolate far beyond the data.** Three points, or a mouse, don't support precise human predictions.
12. **Match the advice to the goal.** Longevity and muscle-gain advice can both be right and still disagree. Pick the goal first.

# Kim
**Author:** Rudyard Kipling | **Published:** 1901



---

## Summary

*Kim* follows Kimball O'Hara, the orphaned son of an Irish soldier, who lives as a street child in Lahore and moves easily between languages, castes and religions—the 'Little Friend of all the World.' He attaches himself as chela (disciple) to a Tibetan Buddhist lama searching for the River of the Arrow, whose waters free one from the Wheel of Life. At the same time, Kim is drawn into the 'Great Game,' the rivalry between Britain and Russia in Central Asia, through the horse-trader Mahbub Ali, the ethnologist-spymaster Colonel Creighton, and the Bengali agent Hurree Chunder Mookerjee.

The novel's two quests—the lama's spiritual search and Kim's training as a spy—run in parallel along the Grand Trunk Road and into the Himalayas, culminating in an encounter with Russian and French agents in the hills. Kim is torn between the two identities and the two loyalties; the book ends with the lama declaring that he has found his River, while Kim's own future in the service of the Raj remains implied.

Published in 1901, *Kim* is widely regarded as Kipling's finest long work. Its richly observed detail of Indian life, its multilingual texture and its love of the land have been praised by readers from many backgrounds, while its assumptions about British rule have made it a focal point of postcolonial criticism.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Panorama of India:** Critics consistently praise the novel's vivid, crowded depiction of the Grand Trunk Road and its range of peoples, faiths and languages; Nirad C. Chaudhuri called it the finest story about India in English.
- **Empire and its fictions:** Edward Said, in his introduction to the Penguin edition and in *Culture and Imperialism*, called it a masterpiece while arguing it presents British rule as natural and unquestioned, with no conflict between Kim's loyalties.
- **Dual quest structure:** The interplay between the lama's spiritual journey and the espionage plot is often discussed as the novel's central tension—between detachment and worldly action.
- **Identity and hybridity:** Scholars read Kim's fluid identity across race, religion and class as both a celebration of cultural mixing and an imperial fantasy of the Englishman who can pass as anyone.
- **Episodic form:** Some critics note that the plot is loose and episodic, with the Great Game storyline thinner than the novel's travelogue and character portraits.

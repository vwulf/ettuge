# The Passage of Power (The Years of Lyndon Johnson, Vol. 4)
**Author:** Robert A. Caro | **Published:** 2012



---

## Summary

*The Passage of Power* begins with Johnson at the height of his Senate power, hesitating to declare for the 1960 Democratic nomination and being outmanoeuvred by John F. Kennedy's campaign. Caro reconstructs the convention drama in which Kennedy offered Johnson the vice presidency, against the fierce opposition of Robert F. Kennedy, whose mutual hatred with Johnson becomes a running thread of the book.

The vice-presidential years are portrayed as a period of marginalisation and despair, with Johnson excluded from key decisions and increasingly the target of Washington mockery and of investigations touching his associate Bobby Baker. Then, on 22 November 1963, the assassination in Dallas makes him president. Caro narrates the hours and weeks that followed in great detail: the swearing-in aboard Air Force One, Johnson's careful handling of the Kennedy staff and family, and his speech to Congress.

Caro argues that Johnson's legislative skill allowed him to break the logjam on Kennedy's tax cut and civil rights bill, setting the stage for the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The book ends in early 1964 with Johnson on the verge of his greatest achievements and the escalation in Vietnam still ahead. It won the National Book Critics Circle Award for biography.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **The transition as drama:** Reviewers widely praised the account of the assassination and transfer of power as among the finest passages in the series.
- **Johnson–RFK feud:** The book's treatment of the Johnson–Robert Kennedy rivalry is regarded as a rich, sometimes revelatory study of personal animosity in politics.
- **Sympathy and severity:** Critics note that Caro is more sympathetic to Johnson here, emphasising his restraint and competence in crisis, while still documenting his insecurities and abuses.
- **Selective emphasis:** Some reviewers questioned the lengthy treatment of the vice-presidency and suggested Caro relies heavily on certain interview sources for the Kennedy-camp perspective.
- **Series context:** Commentators frame it as a bridge toward the still-unpublished final volume on the presidency, leaving the major questions of Vietnam and the Great Society unresolved.

# Bunkai-Jutsu: The Practical Application of Karate Kata
**Author:** Iain Abernethy | **Published:** 2002



---

## Summary

*Bunkai-Jutsu* by British karate instructor Iain Abernethy argues that traditional karate kata were designed as catalogs of effective civilian self-protection techniques, and that the common practice of interpreting them as blocks against long-range punches and kicks misses their meaning.

Abernethy proposes rules for analyzing kata: assume the opponent is at close range, interpret movements in the context of real violence (grabs, headbutts, haymaker punches), recognize that "blocks" are often strikes, locks or throws, and treat the non-striking hand as active. He draws on the writings of early masters such as Itosu and Funakoshi to support the view that kata contain grappling, throws and strikes to vulnerable points.

The book also discusses how to train applications with partners, progressing to live drills, so that kata practice connects to functional skill.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Influence:** The book is widely regarded as a key text in the modern movement toward practical, self-defense-oriented kata application.
- **Clear framework:** Reviewers credit Abernethy's analytical rules as a usable method for practitioners of any style.
- **Debate over intent:** Critics and traditionalists note that claims about what kata creators intended are partly reconstruction and cannot be fully proven.
- **Limited formal criticism:** Its reception is documented mainly in martial-arts magazines and practitioner reviews.

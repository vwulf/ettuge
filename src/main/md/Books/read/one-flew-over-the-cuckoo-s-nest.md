# One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest
**Author:** Ken Kesey | **Published:** 1962



---

## Summary

Randle Patrick McMurphy, a convicted brawler who has faked insanity to escape a prison work farm, arrives on a ward run by Nurse Ratched, who governs her patients through routine, humiliation and the quiet threat of electroshock and lobotomy. McMurphy's gambling, laughter and defiance begin to wake the men up; he organises a fishing trip, a basketball team and finally an illicit party. The cost of his rebellion is total: after he attacks Ratched he is lobotomised, and Bromden, now restored to himself, smothers McMurphy's body and escapes.

Kesey drew on his work as a night aide at a veterans' hospital in Menlo Park and on his participation in government-sponsored drug experiments. The narration through Bromden, who perceives society as a 'Combine' and the ward as a machine, gives the book its hallucinatory force and its broader argument about a society that grinds individuals into conformity.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Anti-institutional allegory:** Critics read the ward as a microcosm of a conformist, managerial America, and the novel as a key text of the 1960s revolt against authority.
- **Bromden's narration:** The choice of a Native American narrator who sees the hidden machinery of control is widely praised as the novel's formal masterstroke and a significant shift from the film, which centres McMurphy.
- **Gender politics:** Feminist critics have pointed to the book's misogyny: the oppressors are largely women (Ratched, the patients' wives and mothers), and McMurphy's liberation is coded as a restoration of masculinity.
- **Race and caricature:** The portrayal of the Black aides has been criticised as racist stereotyping, a point raised in later reassessments.
- **Psychiatry and its critics:** The book is linked to the anti-psychiatry and deinstitutionalisation debates of its time, though historians caution against treating it as a factual portrait of mental health care.

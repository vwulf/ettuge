# Unweaving the Rainbow: Science, Delusion and the Appetite for Wonder
**Author:** Richard Dawkins | **Published:** 1998



---

## Summary

The title comes from Keats's poem *Lamia*, which laments that philosophy will "unweave a rainbow." Dawkins argues the opposite: that understanding how light, sound, the stars or living things actually work deepens the sense of wonder rather than destroying it. He explores topics such as the physics of light and spectra, barcodes in nature and DNA fingerprinting, and the workings of the brain.

He also goes after what he sees as the misuse of the appetite for wonder: astrology, psychic claims and credulity about coincidences, as well as scientists whose poetic metaphors he considers misleading. Later chapters return to evolution and the idea of genes and the brain as models of the world.

The book's spirit is close to Carl Sagan's, combining celebration of science with skepticism, and it is one of Dawkins's most literary works.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Celebration of science:** Reviewers praised its eloquent case that science is a source of beauty and wonder.
- **Prose:** It is often cited as among Dawkins's most lyrical writing.
- **Combative digressions:** Critics found the attacks on astrology and paranormal belief easy targets and at odds with the book's poetic aims.
- **Disputes with scientists:** His criticism of other scientists' writing, including Stephen Jay Gould's, was seen by some as partisan.
- **Loose structure:** Some reviewers felt the book is a collection of essays rather than a sustained argument.

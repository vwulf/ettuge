# Steppenwolf
**Author:** Hermann Hesse | **Published:** 1927



---

## Summary

The novel presents itself as Harry Haller's records, found by the nephew of his landlady. Haller is cultured, alienated and contemptuous of bourgeois life yet unable to leave it, and he contemplates suicide. He comes across a 'Treatise on the Steppenwolf' that diagnoses him: he is not two beings, man and wolf, but a multitude of selves. Through Hermine, a young woman who seems to know him better than he knows himself, he meets the saxophonist Pablo and the sensual Maria and learns to dance, laugh and live in the body. The book climaxes in Pablo's Magic Theatre, a drug-fuelled sequence of doors and scenes in which Haller confronts the fragments of his personality, fails the test by 'killing' Hermine, and is told he must learn to laugh, as the Immortals Mozart and Goethe do.

Written during a personal crisis in Hesse's life, the novel reflects his engagement with Jungian psychoanalysis and his critique of Weimar-era German culture, nationalism and the coming war.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Multiplicity of the self:** Critics see its core idea, that personality is plural rather than dual, as a striking anticipation of later psychological and postmodern thought.
- **Misread success:** Hesse himself complained that the book was often misunderstood as a book of despair; he insisted the Immortals and humour point toward healing. Its 1960s cult status, linked to psychedelic culture, is often cited as such a misreading.
- **Formal experiment:** The embedded treatise and the surreal Magic Theatre make it Hesse's most structurally adventurous work.
- **Cultural critique:** Its satire of bourgeois complacency and German militarism gives it historical weight beyond its personal crisis.
- **Self-absorption:** Detractors find Haller's introspection overwrought and the novel's women more symbolic than real.

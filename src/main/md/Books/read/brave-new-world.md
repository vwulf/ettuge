# Brave New World
**Author:** Aldous Huxley | **Published:** 1932



---

## Summary

*Brave New World* opens in the Central London Hatchery and Conditioning Centre, where human embryos are produced and graded into Alphas, Betas, Gammas, Deltas and Epsilons, then conditioned by sleep-teaching and reflex training to love their station. Stability is the highest value: family, religion, art and history have been abolished, promiscuity is encouraged, and any unhappiness is dissolved with soma. Bernard Marx, an Alpha who feels out of place, visits a New Mexico reservation with Lenina Crowne and brings back John, raised on the reservation and educated on a stray volume of Shakespeare.

John's encounter with the World State sets his Shakespearean ideals of love, suffering and dignity against its frictionless consumerism. In the long debate with the World Controller Mustapha Mond, Mond defends the trade of truth, beauty and freedom for comfort and stability, and John claims 'the right to be unhappy'. The novel ends in tragedy, with John unable to live either inside or outside the society. Huxley returned to its themes in the essay collection *Brave New World Revisited* (1958) and offered a utopian counterpart in *Island* (1962).

## Critical Takeaways

- **Control through pleasure:** Critics routinely contrast Huxley's vision with Orwell's: where *1984* imagines domination by pain and surveillance, Huxley imagines a population that loves its servitude, an idea many commentators (notably Neil Postman) found more prophetic of consumer and media culture.
- **Science and technology:** The novel's reproductive technology, conditioning and pharmacology are frequently cited in modern bioethics debates about genetic engineering and mood-altering drugs.
- **Satire of Fordism:** The worship of Henry Ford and mass production reflects Huxley's alarm at American consumer capitalism and at the efficiency ideologies of the 1920s.
- **Limits of the Savage:** Critics note that John is not a straightforward hero; his alternative is shaped by religious self-punishment and a narrow reading of Shakespeare, so the book offers no comfortable escape from its dilemma.
- **Standing and controversy:** It is a fixture of best-novel lists and school curricula, and has also been repeatedly challenged or banned for its sexual content and treatment of religion.

# Of Mice and Men
**Author:** John Steinbeck | **Published:** 1937



---

## Summary

George Milton and Lennie Small travel together from job to job; Lennie, immensely strong but intellectually disabled, keeps getting them into trouble by touching soft things he cannot stop holding. At a ranch near Soledad they share their dream of a few acres where Lennie can tend rabbits, and for a moment it seems within reach when the old swamper Candy offers his savings. But Lennie accidentally kills the wife of the boss's son Curley, and George, to spare him a lynching, shoots him while describing the farm one last time.

Steinbeck wrote the book as a 'play-novelette' that could be staged almost directly, and it was adapted for Broadway the same year. Its title comes from Robert Burns's line about the best-laid schemes of mice and men going astray. Its spare scenes give voice to the isolated men of the ranch, Crooks, Candy and Curley's wife among them, each excluded and lonely in a different way.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Loneliness and companionship:** Critics see the George and Lennie bond, rare among the ranch's isolated workers, as the novel's emotional core and its critique of an economy that keeps men rootless.
- **The American Dream:** The farm is widely read as a version of the American Dream that the novel shows to be unattainable for the dispossessed.
- **Dramatic economy:** Its play-like structure, with scenes in a few settings and heavy foreshadowing (Candy's dog), is praised for tautness and criticised by some as schematic.
- **Frequently challenged:** It is among the most often banned or challenged books in American schools, citing profanity, racial slurs and its ending.
- **Portrayal of disability and women:** Later critics have examined its depiction of Lennie and of Curley's wife, who is never given a name.

# The Odessa File
**Author:** Frederick Forsyth | **Published:** 1972



---

## Summary

In November 1963, Peter Miller, a freelance crime reporter in Hamburg, obtains the diary of Salomon Tauber, an elderly Jewish survivor of the Riga ghetto who has just taken his own life. The diary describes the crimes of the camp commandant Eduard Roschmann, the "Butcher of Riga." Miller resolves to find Roschmann, and his investigation leads him to ODESSA, a secret organization of former SS members that shields its own and has infiltrated postwar German institutions.

Miller is recruited by Israeli agents, trained to pass as a former SS man, and infiltrates the network, while ODESSA is in turn involved in a plot to help Egypt develop rockets aimed at Israel. The story builds to a confrontation with Roschmann and a final revelation about Miller's personal reason for the hunt.

Like *The Day of the Jackal*, the novel weaves real people and historical detail into fiction. Roschmann was a real war criminal, and the 1974 film adaptation is credited with contributing to his being recognized in South America in the 1970s.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Documentary realism:** Critics praised Forsyth's research and procedural detail, which give the thriller the texture of reportage.
- **Postwar reckoning:** The novel was notable for confronting West Germany's incomplete denazification at a time when it was a sensitive subject.
- **History versus fiction:** Historians have questioned how organized and powerful the real ODESSA network was; the novel presents it more coherently than the evidence supports.
- **Thin characters:** As with much of Forsyth's work, reviewers find the characterization functional and secondary to plot.
- **Real-world impact:** The story and its film are often cited for drawing public attention to Roschmann himself.

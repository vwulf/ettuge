# Jonathan Livingston Seagull
**Author:** Richard Bach | **Published:** 1970



---

## Summary

*Jonathan Livingston Seagull* follows a gull who is bored by the flock's daily scramble for scraps and devotes himself instead to learning to fly faster and better. His obsession gets him banished as irresponsible. He then ascends to another plane where gulls who share his love of flight learn from an elder, Chiang, that perfect speed is 'being there' and that the limits of the body are limits of thought. Jonathan eventually returns to the flock to teach outcasts like himself, and the final part shows his teaching beginning to harden into reverence for him as a figure.

Illustrated with Russell Munson's photographs of gulls, the slim book was rejected by many publishers, then became one of the bestselling books of the 1970s in the US. Its message, that self-perfection and freedom lie in transcending conformity and self-imposed limits, connected with the era's interest in Eastern spirituality and personal growth. A 1973 film adaptation followed, and in 2014 Bach published an expanded edition with a fourth part.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Phenomenon over literature:** Critics generally treat it as a cultural phenomenon rather than a literary achievement; its sales and influence on the self-help and New Age culture of the 1970s are its main significance.
- **Simplicity as strength and weakness:** Admirers value its parable form and uplifting message; detractors found it sentimental, platitudinous and thin.
- **Individualism:** Commentators note the book's strongly individualist ethic, the exceptional self versus the conformist flock, which some read as inspiring and others as smug.
- **Spiritual borrowings:** Its ideas of transcending the body and achieving perfection through practice echo Christian Science, Buddhist and other mystical traditions in a loose, eclectic way.

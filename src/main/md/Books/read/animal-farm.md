# Animal Farm
**Author:** George Orwell | **Published:** 1945



---

## Summary

Inspired by old Major's vision of a world without human masters, the animals drive out Mr. Jones and rename the place Animal Farm, governed by the Seven Commandments of Animalism. The pigs, cleverest of the animals, take charge. Snowball, an idealist planner, is driven out by Napoleon and his trained dogs; the propagandist Squealer rewrites history and the commandments; the loyal cart-horse Boxer works himself to exhaustion and is sold to the knacker. By the end the pigs walk on two legs, the single commandment left reads 'All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others', and the other animals can no longer tell pig from man.

Orwell wrote the book during the Second World War, when the Soviet Union was a British ally, and had trouble finding a publisher. It maps closely onto Soviet history: Major as Marx or Lenin, Napoleon as Stalin, Snowball as Trotsky, the windmill as the Five-Year Plans. Its clarity and economy made it a staple of schools and of Cold War cultural politics.

---

## Critical Takeaways

- **Allegory of revolution betrayed:** Critics read it as an exact satire of Stalinism and, more broadly, of how revolutions are corrupted by those who seize power.
- **Language and propaganda:** Squealer's manipulation of language and memory anticipates the themes of *Nineteen Eighty-Four*.
- **Orwell's politics:** Orwell, a democratic socialist, intended it as a critique of Soviet totalitarianism, not of socialism itself; its Cold War use as anti-communist propaganda complicated that reading.
- **Fable form:** Its plain style and fairy-story structure ('A Fairy Story' was its original subtitle) are seen as the source of its universality.
- **Complicity of the governed:** Critics also stress the role of the other animals' credulity and passivity in enabling tyranny.

# Reading the sources

Short notes on the books I have read closely, recording what each one adds beyond the other pages in this series. Each note gives the claim first, then how it works and what is still open.

## Motobu Chōki, *Watashi no Karate-jutsu* (McCarthy and Quast translations)

**Motobu's quarrel with Itosu was about order, not just which techniques work in a fight.**
In the Itosu method, kata is the main way karate is passed on, and the student is left to work out applications from it later. Motobu held that this gets the order backwards: kata distils application only if application has been trained directly, and if it hasn't, application never catches up. On this reading, modernization made kata explicit and left application implicit. What is still open is how much of that reversal Matsubayashi took on and how much Nagamine resisted.
→ Catalog: [My Art of Karate](../Books/influential/my-art-of-karate) · [Martial Artists of Ryukyu](../Books/read/martial-artists-of-ryukyu)

## Shōshin Nagamine, *The Essence of Okinawan Karate-Dō* (1976)

**Nagamine's disagreement with the modernizers is in what he kept in the curriculum, not in what he wrote.**
Motobu could criticize openly. Nagamine wrote in postwar Okinawa, shortly after the 1972 reversion, within the *honne/tatemae* (本音/建前) register, so *Essence* stays formally respectful and names no one. The dissent shows in three choices: Motobu's Naihanchi as the foundation kata, Motobu's approval cited as the basis for the yakusoku kumite, and the full Tomari-te stream kept intact. What is still open is whether a specifically Okinawan term describes this kind of indirection better than the generic Japanese pair.
→ Catalog: [The Essence of Okinawan Karate-Do](../Books/influential/essence-of-okinawan-karate-do)

## Shōshin Nagamine, *Tales of Okinawa's Great Masters* (trans. McCarthy)

***Tales* can be read as the narrative half of the same argument: a defense of the older masters against their disappearance from the mainland story.**
*Essence* preserves the older tradition in practice. *Tales* preserves the people, and gives Motobu, Kyan and Matsumora their full weight instead of treating them as a prelude to the Japanese styles. Read alongside *Essence*, the choice of who gets a chapter becomes part of the position. What is still open is separating what this framing tells us about Nagamine's intent from the book's documented hagiographic layer.
→ Catalog: [Tales of Okinawa's Great Masters](../Books/read/tales-of-okinawa-s-great-masters)

## Andreas Quast: the *hōgen* layer

**A kata's name records how it was transmitted. Kata taught in schools got Sino-Japanese names, and kata passed down informally kept Okinawan ones.**
The prefectural ban on Okinawan speech (*hōgen*, 方言) in schools left school-taught kata with kanji names such as Gojūshiho (五十四歩) and no surviving native name. Older kata that moved through informal Okinawan-speaking channels kept katakana names that point to non-Japanese origins. This adds a third layer of suppression beneath the political and stylistic ones above. What is still open is the exact passage in Quast that supports this, so the argument can be cited properly.
→ Catalog: [Karate 1.0](../Books/influential/karate-1-0)

## Andreas Quast: kobudō name attestations

**The attested Okinawan forms of kobudō kata names show sound rules that compete by dialect layer rather than following one derivation.**
Forms such as *Shiramachi* (Shiromatsu, tsu→chi), *Shirataru* (Shirotarō, ō→u) and the pair *Shiishi* / *Shuuji* (Shūshi) test the Nihongo→Uchinaaguchi correspondences against actual usage. When two orderings of the same rules both appear in the record, the likeliest explanation is two transmission lines or two dialects, not an error in either. Kanji stay fixed across both readings as semantic anchors, and the reading changes the register. What is still open is mapping each variant to its lineage.
→ Catalog: [Bo, Vol. 1](../Books/read/bo)
